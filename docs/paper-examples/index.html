An Early-Warning Queue for Organic Click Decline: Client-Grouped and Time-Forward Evaluation of a Decision-Support Ranker

Worked example paper — written as an intern would submit it. Companion artifact to the Week 7 session.

Abstract

Problem. A central content team has a fifty-row monthly portfolio review budget; the decision this system supports is the order of that review, so precision@50 was fixed before training. Setting. The source is a 78,835,655-row pseudonymized warehouse release. Development evaluation uses 16,513 eligible content items across 36 clients, with validation grouped by client. Approach. We compare an audited rule with logistic regression, random forest and an eleven-feature HistGradientBoosting model on identical folds. Evidence. HistGradientBoosting has the higher mean in this evaluation: 0.504 ± 0.211 precision@50 (mean ± SD across five client-grouped folds) versus the rule's 0.384 ± 0.182; it is higher in 3 folds and lower in 2. A separate revision-pinned time-forward holdout reaches 0.62. An earlier, separate nine-feature random-forest boundary demonstration moves from 0.996 with random rows to 0.496 with client grouping. In sealed June (19,432 rows, 37 clients), the base rate is 0.655, the rule 0.380, the model 0.740, and the naive list 0.780. After labels arrived, the proposed base-rate alarm would have paused the next release. Scope. One month exposes a material shift but is thin temporal evidence; monthly refitting remains a policy to validate, and no causal claim about refresh impact is made. Code, seeds, environment and a result-to-notebook map are provided.

1  Introduction and claims

A page that loses organic clicks silently costs traffic until someone notices. The practical decision is not "will this page decline?" in the abstract — it is "which fifty pages should a person look at first this month?" This paper reports a decision-support ranker for that queue and evaluates it the way it would be used: grouped by client, walked forward in time, and finally tested once on a sealed month.

We make three testable claims:

  1. C1. In five client-grouped folds, HistGradientBoosting has the higher observed mean at K ≤ 75 and does not have it past K ≈ 100; it is higher than the rule in three p@50 folds and lower in two (§6.1, Figure 1).
  2. C2. The evaluation boundary is load-bearing: in an earlier, separate nine-feature random-forest demonstration, random rows score 0.996 and client grouping 0.496; experiment identity and grouping must travel with the number (§6.2).
  3. C3. One sealed month produces a materially different label mix. The post-label outcome check in the proposed monitoring policy would pause the next release for review (§6.4).

Contributions: a leakage-controlled feature frame and label; an audited rule baseline strong enough to be worth beating; a grouped + time-forward + sealed-month evaluation design; and a deployment-shaped error analysis whose failure modes were promoted to implementation-labeled policy in the accompanying action playbook.

2  Related context

The system sits between two established practices. Rule systems encode judgment transparently and are a useful first system; our baseline is exactly such a rule, audited on held-out data before any model existed. This follows Google's baseline-first guidance. For the learned comparator, we use scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting classifier through its probabilities rather than a bespoke ranking objective, because the decision only needs an ordering. We enforce the client boundary with GroupKFold: clients do not cross the train/test boundary. We do not claim methodological novelty; the contribution is the evaluation design and its deployment reading.

3  Data and task

3.1  Unit, source, period

One observation is one content item for one client at a monthly decision point. Source: the pseudonymized FlyRank internship warehouse release, 78,835,655 daily fact rows spanning 2025-01-27 to 2026-06-30. Warehouse inventory contains 104 client records and 519,606 content records; the daily fact source covers 70 clients and 427,292 content items. The development evaluation frame is narrower: 16,513 eligible items across 36 clients. Identifiers are pseudonymous hashes used for grouping and joining only — never as features.

3.2  Label

label_declined = (May clicks < 0.8 × April clicks) — an observed outcome in a window strictly after every feature window. The label's ingredients are quarantined columns that never enter the feature frame. Development base rate: 0.4162.

3.3  Eligibility and exclusions

Eligible rows: ≥1,000 impressions across the three feature months and ≥10 last-feature-month clicks — floors that keep the label meaningful. Excluded outright: engagement/AI columns (sparse behind a three-valued availability flag; filtered with IS TRUE everywhere), the raw position-sum ingredient, the partition key, and last_optimized_date (a decision someone already made). The starter dataset's label-derived fields (trend_direction, trend_pct) never appear.

3.4  Leakage controls

Three boundaries, each tested in code: (i) feature windows end strictly before the label window; (ii) clients never cross fold boundaries; (iii) the sealed month (June 2026) was read exactly once, by one notebook cell, after all development was frozen. A deliberately leaky client-derived feature is kept as a test fixture; the test suite asserts that the production feature frame refuses it.

3.5  Dataset limitations

All frames keep clients alive in the outcome month (a churn-censoring choice); GSC-visible pages only, which is a minority of raw panel rows; no engagement or conversion outcomes. Each limitation reappears in §9.

4  Baselines and model

The baseline ladder runs from trivial to operational. The frozen rule came out of a signal audit performed before modeling: established pages whose last feature month ran 3.3–7× their first declined at 0.689 against the 0.4162 base; beyond 7× the rate is 0.503. Points: +3 spike, +2 extreme, +1 rising; ties broken by last-month clicks. The audit also produced an anti-baseline: the intuitive "already dipping" watchlist declines at 0.332 — below base — consistent with month-to-month mean reversion in this frame. We report it because a paper that only shows the baselines it beat invites the question of the ones it hid.

Features (11, all knowable at the decision instant): impr_90d, clicks_90d, impr_last, clicks_last, clicks_first, clicks_mid, momentum, ctr_90d, wavg_position, active_days, is_new. Models: logistic regression, random forest, HistGradientBoosting. Their fixed configurations are declared in the reproduction notebook; the proposed model is used through predicted probabilities as a ranking score. Seed 42 for every stochastic component.

5  Experimental design

Primary metric. precision@50, fixed before training: the editor's capacity is the queue's head, so metric and decision coincide. Secondary: precision@K for K ∈ {10…200}, grouped AUC (reported in the repository), per-client medians.

Splits. (i) GroupKFold(5) by client — uncertainty below is the mean ± SD of the per-fold precision@50 across those five folds; folds differ by client composition, so the SD reflects client heterogeneity as well as sampling noise. (ii) One revision-pinned time-forward holdout: train at the 2026-04-01 decision point, evaluate unchanged at 2026-05-01. (iii) One sealed-month evaluation (June 2026), computed exactly once. Pooled cross-fold probabilities are reported only after within-fold rank normalization — pooling raw probabilities flipped a conclusion in an early draft and is documented in the repository as a trap.

6.2  The boundary experiment

This is an earlier, separate nine-feature random-forest experiment: random rows score 0.996; client grouping, 0.496. Within that demonstration, only the split changes. We keep it as boundary evidence (C2), not as if it were the main eleven-feature HistGB result.

6.3  Error analysis, promoted to policy

Confident false positives concentrate in durable-trend risers (mid month already ≥1.5× the first): momentum without reversion. Confident false negatives are no-warning decliners — flat histories that drop. Both became executable rules in the action playbook (risers route to monitor_only, never a rewrite; flat-history rows carry no rule reason and route to human judgment), which is where an error analysis should end up rather than in an appendix. Position shows little separation for this label in this dataset (spread 0.04 across bands) — a negative result we keep because it prunes a plausible feature story.

6.4  Temporal evaluation and the sealed month

Time-forward (train 2026-04-01 → evaluate unchanged 2026-05-01): 0.62 precision@50. In sealed June (19,432 eligible items, 37 clients), the base rate is 0.655 (dev: 0.416), the naive "already dipping" list is 0.780, the frozen rule at 0.380, the dev-trained model at 0.740. The model is 0.085 above the June base but behind the naive list; the rule is far below the new base. Once June outcomes exist, the proposed outcome-drift check (base rate outside 0.416 ± 0.10) would trip at +0.239 and pause the next release pending review (C3).

7  Failure modes and robustness

One sealed month exposes a material regime shift; more windows are needed to estimate how often this happens. The reference notebook implements a PSI test harness, not a production monitor. The proposed policy separates pre-release population/feature checks from post-label outcome and model-versus-rule checks; post-label checks can pause the next release. Monthly refitting and quarterly band review are policies to validate, not implemented mitigations.

8  Engineering considerations

Batch monthly scoring; no serving infrastructure. A full rebuild — frame, folds, models, figures — runs in minutes on a laptop from cached scans (first warehouse scan is the only heavy step). Inference cost is negligible next to the editor-hours the queue allocates, which is the correct cost frame for this system. The test suite covers the paths whose failure would invalidate the paper: split integrity, feature-window boundaries, schema floors, and the metric implementation.

9  Limitations

  • One walk-forward month and one sealed month; a longer multi-window backtest is the highest-value missing experiment.
  • Results vary substantially across held-out-client folds (p@50 0.16–0.70); this five-fold sample does not establish reliable superiority.
  • Live-clients join censors churned clients; results describe surviving portfolios.
  • No causal claim: nothing here shows that refreshing a flagged page changes its outcome — that requires an experiment this project did not run.
  • GSC-visible, above-floor pages only; absence from the queue is not evidence of health.

10  Conclusion

Under five client-grouped folds, a small gradient-boosted ranker has the higher observed mean at the head of the queue, but not past it, and it is higher in only three of five p@50 folds. The separate boundary experiment shows why grouping matters. One sealed month motivates a monitoring policy whose production integration and refit cadence still need validation. The evidence supports testing an ordering of human attention. It does not support automating actions.

The problem, and who it's for

A central content team has a fifty-page monthly review budget across the portfolio. Per-client slices are a separate reporting view. The real decision is an ordering problem: of everything we could inspect, what do we inspect first? I fixed precision@50 before touching a model so metric and decision capacity describe the same queue.

Data and the split that matters

Source release: 78,835,655 pseudonymized daily fact rows; warehouse inventory has 104 client records and 519,606 content records, while daily facts cover 70 clients and 427,292 items. Evaluation: 16,513 eligible pages across 36 clients; label = "next-month clicks fell below 80% of this month" (base rate 0.416). I split by client, not by row. In an earlier, separate nine-feature random-forest boundary demonstration, random rows scored 0.996 and client grouping 0.496. I reported the grouped number with that experiment's identity because it matches the boundary the system faces.

Baseline first — and an anti-baseline

Before any ML, I audited signals and froze a transparent rule: pages whose month ran 3.3–7× their baseline decline 69% of the time (base: 42%). That rule — not a strawman — is what the model has to beat. The audit also killed the obvious idea: the "already dipping" watchlist everyone would build first declines at only 33%, below base, consistent with mean reversion in this frame. Knowing why the intuitive solution fails is what made the ML worth testing.

Where it fails — the two patterns that became policy

  • High-score false positives: pages on a durable upward trend. The queue routes them to monitor_only — never a rewrite.
  • High-score false negatives: flat histories that drop with no warning. The rule can't name them, so they route to explicit human judgment instead of pretending the model covers them.

A shifted sealed month — and the policy it motivates

Among 19,432 eligible pages across 37 clients, June's base rate rises from 0.416 to 0.655. The frozen rule scores 0.380; the model scores 0.740, about 0.085 above June base but behind the naive list at 0.780. After labels arrive, the proposed base-rate alarm would trip at +0.239 and pause the next release pending review. That is policy behavior, not a claim that a deployed monitor stopped already-issued actions.

The system around the model

Every action in the output queue carries a human-readable reason code from the audited rule; the model only supplies the order. Five policies have explicit status: three are asserted in this notebook, below-floor exclusions are upstream, and recently-optimized exclusion needs a proposed deployment-time join. The PSI harness is implemented; the other monitoring checks are proposed policy. Tests in the reference notebooks cover the paths whose failure would invalidate the results: split integrity, feature-window leakage, schema, metrics.

Limitations, plainly

One walk-forward month is thin temporal evidence. Results describe clients that stayed alive in the outcome month. The offline metric evaluates an ordering; I did not measure whether acting on the queue changes outcomes — that needs an experiment. Past ~100 rows, the rule has the higher observed mean in this evaluation.

What I would test next

  1. A rolling multi-month backtest — the single highest-value missing experiment.
  2. A calibration pass so scores could support thresholds, not just ordering.
  3. A controlled refresh experiment on queue-flagged pages to measure real impact.

Why this matters

Many SEO teams face the same meeting: traffic is drifting somewhere in a big content portfolio, and someone has to decide which pages get attention. In this worked example, capacity is fifty pages per month across one central queue. So the useful output is not a prediction score. It is a defensible ordering of the review list, with a reason on every row a human can inspect.

What we measured

One row = one page on one client site at a monthly decision point. "Declined" = next-month clicks fell below 80% of this month's. In the development month, 42% of eligible pages declined — that number matters, because any method should be read against the random-selection expectation from that base.

Data reality. This is Search-Console-visible data with eligibility floors (≥1,000 impressions over the window, ≥10 recent clicks). Search Console exports omit or anonymize some query rows and are coverage constrained; small pages are noisy, and engagement metrics were excluded as too sparse to trust. Absence from this queue is not evidence a page is healthy. See Google's performance-data documentation.

The method, in plain English

First a baseline rule, built by checking signals one at a time: in this development frame, mid-band spikes often preceded decline, so "spiked recently" earns points, biggest spikes get a verification step, and ties break by recent clicks. Transparent and auditable: on the full development frame, half of its top-50 picks decline; the grouped-fold mean reported below is lower.

Then a small model (gradient-boosted trees — think: many small if-then rules voting) over eleven features a page already has at decision time: impressions, clicks, click-through, position, coverage days, a new-page indicator, and momentum. The model does one job: it orders the queue. The rule keeps its job too: it explains each row. Model = order, rule = reason.

Glossary in one breath — base rate: how often decline happens anyway · precision@50: of the first 50 picks, how many were right · client-grouped test: we never test on a site the model trained on · leakage: letting tomorrow's answer sneak into today's features (we test for it in code).

Did it work?

On client-grouped folds, the model's p@50 mean is 0.504 and the rule's is 0.384 (random-selection expectation 0.416). The model is higher in 3 held-out-client folds and lower in 2; its values span 0.16–0.70. The central queue can also be sliced by client for ownership, but that is a reporting view, not a change to p@50.

Two simple ideas did not separate this target well: the "biggest recent drop" watchlist is below the development base (33% vs 42%), and average position shows little separation for this label (spread 0.04 across bands). Validate them before using either as a primary ranking.

The month the label mix shifted

Among 19,432 eligible pages across 37 clients, the decline-label base rate rises from 42% to 66%. The frozen rule scores 38%, the naive drop-list 78%, and the model 74%. The model remains about 0.085 above June base but behind the naive list; the rule falls far below base.

Once June outcomes are available, the proposed "base rate outside the development band" check would trip at +0.239 and pause the next release pending review. This is a policy illustration, not a claim that a deployed monitor stopped already-issued actions.

What to do with this

Finding. Pages that spiked to 3.3–7× their baseline month decline 69% of the time (portfolio base: 42%).

What this means. In this development frame, mid-band spikes often preceded decline. These pages merit review, not an automatic edit.

Action. Review intent match, verify the pattern, and form a hypothesis; the queue labels these review_before_revert.

Do not. Do not treat the spike as proof the page 'works' — and do not bulk-rewrite everything that spiked.

Measure. In a controlled pilot, compare later clicks with comparable unreviewed spikes.

Finding. The intuitive “already dipping” watchlist declines at only 33% — below the 42% base rate.

What this means. In this development month, many recent drops reversed. The natural drop-list is an anti-signal for this label and frame.

Action. Do not use 'biggest recent drop' as the primary ranking without validating it; keep the list as context.

Do not. Do not conclude dipping pages never matter — some are the no-warning decliners the model also misses.

Measure. Compare review outcomes from the model-ordered queue against the old drop-sorted list on the same month.

Finding. Pages already rising for two straight months (≥1.5× their base) recur among high-score false positives.

What this means. In this frame, persistent momentum often continued; editing these pages without review risks disrupting a working trend.

Action. The queue routes them to monitor_only automatically. A human sees them; nobody edits them.

Do not. Do not 'optimize' a page mid-climb because a score is high — the score is high because the pattern is unusual.

Measure. Track that monitor_only pages keep growing untouched; any editor override gets logged.

What we refused to automate: no auto-rewrites, ever; new pages stay out of the queue (their stats are artifacts of small history); spikes above 20× route to a tracking check before anyone touches content — at that size it may be instrumentation or a campaign rather than audience behavior. Three gates are asserted here; below-floor exclusion is upstream; recently-optimized exclusion is a proposed deployment join.

The value, as a scenario — not a promise

Derived arithmetic, stated with its assumptions: at 50 reviews a month, precision 0.504 vs 0.384 means roughly 6 more labeled decliners in a 50-row batch ((0.504 − 0.384) × 50 ≈ 6). This is descriptive fold-mean arithmetic from the five-fold evaluation, not a forecast of future impact.

Assumptions this depends on: your portfolio behaves like this one (it may not); review capacity is really the bottleneck; and catching a decliner early is worth an editor-hour. What it does not include: any claim about traffic recovered — that requires a controlled test, which is the natural next study.

Limits — including the strongest counterargument

This is one portfolio, one label definition, and only one truly out-of-time month. The strongest counterargument to the whole approach: since clicks mean-revert, many "decliners" recover untouched, so a queue that finds decliners may partly find volatility. We agree — which is exactly why the recommended workflow is score → human review → hypothesis → controlled test, and why nothing here claims causality. Every number above is observed or measured; the decisions it supports are ordering decisions.
